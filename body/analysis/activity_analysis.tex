\subsubsection{Configuration Effects}\label{subsubsec:configuration_effects}

The data show that the density and abundance estimates obtained using DPT are greater than those
using Depth Anything.
Additionally, for each of these distance estimation models, the density and abundance estimates obtained using the
bounding box detection method are also generally greater than those using segmentation detection.
This gives the overall trend of a decrease in estimates from MANUAL $\rightarrow$ DPT/BBOX $\rightarrow$
DPT/SEG $\rightarrow$ DA/BBOX $\rightarrow$ DA/SEG\@.
This trend can be rationalised by considering how distance influences estimated density.

In essence, density estimation hinges on fitting a detection function, $g(r)$, to the distribution
of observed distances.
The gives the probability that a particular individual will be detected at a given distance, giving
a measure of how detection probability decays with distance.
This relies on the assumption that there is a  100\% probability of individuals being detected at a
distance of zero meters.
When distances are overestimated, the detection function becomes elongated such that detection
probability decays at a slower rate with respect to distance owing to the elongated function accounting
for an increased probability of detection at far distances (Figure~\ref{fig:detection_function}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{body/analysis/assets/activity/detection_function}
    \caption{Graph showing how the distribution of distance estimates affects the fitting of a
    detection function. The blue line represents a detection function fitted with distances
    of lower values while the red line represents one fitted with larger values, resulting in
    the area under these curves being different in size.}
    \label{fig:detection_function}
\end{figure}

Point transect density, $D$, is given by the equation
\[
    D = \dfrac{n}{2k\pi \cdot \displaystyle \int_{0}^{w} r \cdot g(r) \, dr}
\]
where $n$ is the number of detected individuals, $k$ is the number of transects and $w$ is the far
truncation distance corresponding to the furthest considered distance\cite{distance_samping}.
It is recognised that the resulting value of integral $\int_{0}^{w} r \cdot g(r) \, dr$ scales
with area under the detection function curve (i.e., $\int_{0}^{w} g(r) \, dr$).
This shows, therefore, that density is inversely proportional to the area under the detection function
curve, demonstrating that systematic overestimation of distance will lead to lower density estimates.

As seen previously in Figure~\ref{tab:overall_errors} (Section~\ref{subsubsec:distance_comparison}),
the $\Delta_{average}$ values (measure of over/under-prediction relative to manual) of these
configurations tend towards overestimation in order of DPT/BBOX $\rightarrow$ DPT/SEG $\rightarrow$
DA/BBOX $\rightarrow$ DA/SEG, where DPT/BBOX gives the lowest overestimate of $\Delta_{average}$ = 0.586
meters and DA/SEG gives the largest overestimate of $\Delta_{average}$ = 2.80 meters.
This observed correlation with the density and abundance estimates seen in Figure~\ref{fig:activity_chart},
in conjunction with the understanding that distance overestimates result in density underestimates, explains
this trend.

\subsubsection{Sample Effects}

In Figure~\ref{fig:activity_chart}, the 'supplemented' and 'model only' data refer to density
and abundance estimates obtained using the manual and automated detection frame samples respectively
(See Section~\ref{subsubsec:formatting}).
It is seen that for all distance estimation pipeline configurations, the model only distances
consistently underestimate density and abundance relative to the corresponding supplemented
data.
This result is unsurprising given the nature of the frame sampling and formatting methods used
to obtain these distance data.

In the case of the supplemented data obtained from the manual sample, the total number of individual
distance estimates (observations) used to estimate population density is exactly equal to the
number of entirely manual distance estimates used to derive a manual estimate (i.e., the conventional
method that is used as a benchmark).
Here, each sampled frame corresponds to exactly one manual annotation supplied in the dataset.
As only the distances associated with detection frames capturing a single individual are model estimated
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:distance_comparison}), all remaining annotations that are not covered are
supplemented with the associated manual estimates.
This includes those corresponding to single-chimp frames where detections where missed by Mega Detector.

In contrast, as the model-only data obtained from the automated sample are not associated with any
specific annotations in the dataset, the number of individual distance estimates used to estimate
density is entirely dependent on the ability of Mega Detector to identify individuals.
The dense forested environment at Taï national park inevitably results in many individuals undetected
in circumstances where the human annotator was indeed able to do so.
When significant occlusion is a factor, the detector is handicapped by the lack of context from
previous and subsequent frames that are available to the human annotator.
Another contributor to this are the instances where two individuals are detected as a single individual.
Cases such as these are most commonly observed where an individual is holding an infant.
Ultimately, these factors result in many observations being unaccounted for with this model only data
(almost a thousand misses in this case) which leads to underestimated density and abundance, given that these
are directly related to the number of detections and that misses also occur at close distances.

\subsubsection{Additional Comments}

\textbf{False Positives}

One might assume that the opposite effect may hold influence over the density estimates to
some degree.
While false positives do occur, they can be considered mostly insignificant since the number
of these cases is dwarfed by many more missed detections, thus constituting minimal influence
on density and abundance estimates.

\vspace{5mm}
\textbf{Bootstraps}

The results of the bootstrapping (Tables~\ref{tab:suplemented_activity} and~\ref{tab:model_only_activity},
Appendix) for the different configurations generally show good stability
with minimal effect on the variance, error and confidence intervals, indicating that the functions
fitted to the distance distributions are suitable and insensitive to random fluctuations in
the data from resampling.
This gives a positive insight into the density and abundance estimates and indicates that the errors
are representative with respect to the distance distribution.\cite{distance_samping}
