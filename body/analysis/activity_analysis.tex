\subsubsection{Configuration Effects}

The data show that the density and abundance estimates obtained using DPT are greater than those
using Depth Anything.
Additionally, for each of these distance estimation models, the activity estimates obtained using the
bounding box detection method are also generally greater than those using segmentation detection.
This gives the overall trend of a decrease in activity from MANUAL $\rightarrow$ DPT/BBOX $\rightarrow$
DPT/SEG $\rightarrow$ DA/BBOX $\rightarrow$ DA/SEG\@.
This trend can be rationalised by considering how distance influences estimated density.

In essence, density estimation hinges on fitting a detection function, $g(x)$, to the distribution
of observed distances.
The gives the probability that a particular individual will be detected at a given distance, giving
a measure of how detection probability decays with distance.
This relies on the assumption that there is a  100\% probability of individuals being detected at a
distance of zero meters.
When distances are overestimated, the detection function becomes elongated such that detection
probability decays at a slower rate with respect to distance owing to the elongated function accounting
for an increased probability of detection at far distances (Figure~\ref{fig:detection_function}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{body/analysis/assets/activity/detection_function}
    \caption{Graph showing how the distribution of distance estimates affects the fitting of a
    detection function. The blue line represents a detection function fitted with distances
    of lower values while the red line represents one fitted with larger values, resulting in
    the area under these curves being different in size.}
    \label{fig:detection_function}
\end{figure}

Density, $D$, is given by the equation
\[
    D = \dfrac{n}{2L \cdot \displaystyle \int_{0}^{w} g(y) \, dy}
\]
where $n$ is the number of detected individuals, $L$ is the transect length and $w$ is the far
truncation distance corresponding to the furthest considered distance\cite{distance_samping}.
This shows that density is inversely proportional to the area under the detection function given
by $\int_{0}^{w} g(y) \, dy$, demonstrating that systematic overestimation of distance will lead
to lower density estimates.

As seen previously in Figure~\ref{tab:overall_errors} (Section~\ref{subsubsec:distance_comparison}),
the $\Delta_{average}$ values (measure of over/under-prediction relative to manual) of these
configurations tend towards overestimation in order of DPT/BBOX $\rightarrow$ DPT/SEG $\rightarrow$
DA/BBOX $\rightarrow$ DA/SEG, where DPT/BBOX gives the lowest overestimate of $\Delta_{average}$ = 0.586
meters and DA/SEG gives the largest overestimate of $\Delta_{average}$ = 2.80 meters.
This observed correlation with the activity estimates seen in Figure~\ref{fig:activity_chart}, in
conjunction with the understanding that distance overestimates result in density underestimates, explains
this trend.
