\section{Evaluation and Conclusions}

The analyses of results obtained using the methodology proposed in this study show great
promise for the future of conservational biomonitoring.
It is shown that application of current state-of-the-art monocular depth estimation
frameworks in conjunction with modern distance sampling can produce estimates of population
density and abundance on the order of those derived in the conventional manner.
Most importantly though, the time and labour commitments necessitated by this methodology
are orders of magnitude lower.
Manual distance estimation across a dataset of the size used in this study may take weeks,
even months, depending on available resources.
The pipeline explored in this study achieves this in hours.
Nevertheless, the methodology must continue to be improved and adapted with the emergence
of novel technologies and models before it can become convention.

\subsection{Roadblocks and Bottlenecks}

\subsubsection{Detection}

This study has found that, when outsourcing distance estimation to an automated pipeline,
resulting estimates for density and abundance consistently fall below those using manual
distances.
However, the greatest barrier that must be overcome here is not error in distance estimates,
although this remains important, but the endeavour of detection itself.
This is certainly true for, at least, this dataset.
It is inevitable that automated detection will result in misses, but the frequency of these
is significant and noticeably impacts estimated density since misses occur over a range
of distances, diminishing the ability to compensate for these with distance sampling.

Fundamentally, detections are missed because of a lack of contextual cues in detection
frames.
Using the manual approach, the human annotator has additional context from subsequent and
previous video frames.
Future studies may attempt to replicate this idea in the distance estimation pipeline.
As it stands, the pipeline of this study uses Mega Detector to identify detections
in extracted frames.
Suppose instead that animal bounding boxes are tracked, continuously, throughout the
duration of the camera trap video.
Here, an object detector, e.g., YOLOv8,\cite{YOLOv8} may be used to generate bounding
boxes while missed detections can be inferred and corresponding bounding boxes predicted
using an object tracking algorithm, e.g., DeepSORT\@.\cite{deepsort}
This approach has already shown impressive results in the biomonitoring space.\cite{detection_pipeline}
These bounding boxes may then be sampled in correspondence to the
extracted frames (i.e. frames used for MDE) and then processed with the depth data in an
equivalent manner.
This method will certainly lower the frequency of misses; however, its effect on subsequent
density and abundance estimates is yet to be seen.

\subsubsection{Calibration Frame Preparation}

Somewhat of a bottleneck in this methodology, is the preparation of the DPT calibration
material (i.e., calibration frames and binary masks).
The time requirement for preparing these is certainly a huge improvement over
manually annotating detection distances, but is still significant, most especially for
binary mask preparation which took approximately a week to complete.
As discussed, the automated masking method in Section~\ref{subsubsec:mask_creation} yields
inconsistent results, highlighting the need for refinement here.
The custom-built calibration frame extractor software, as seen in Section~\ref{fig:frame_extractor},
proved to be of great success, however, allowing for all raw calibration frames to be extracted at a
significantly increased rate.

\subsubsection{Density / Abundance Pre-Processing}

One might imagine that the pre-processing of distance observation/estimation data for use in the population
density and abundance estimation script would also constitute somewhat of a bottleneck (i.e., truncation,
binning, calculating wellness of fit).
While this does still require some manual human input, it is generally the view of practitioners that
the primary contemporary concern is to overcome the distance estimation bottleneck, as the labour
requirements of pre-processing are but a fraction of those required for distance estimation.

\subsection{Optimising Estimates}

\subsubsection{MDE Models}

When applied to this dataset, DPT outperforms Depth Anything with regard to absolute error of
distance estimates.
Additionally, while both models over-predict distance to some degree, the skew is much more
neutral in the case of DPT, in comparison to Depth Anything, which significantly over-predicts.
As explained in Section~\ref{subsubsec:configuration_effects}, this leads to DPT being the
better choice of distance estimation model for achieving density and abundance results that are
most reflective of the ground-truth.
At a high level, this makes sense given that DPT output is specifically calibrated
to each individual camera location and is therefore a better description of the environment.
Future studies may show interesting results if a similar reference frame style calibration
were to be applied to Depth Anything.
This has the potential to produce even better estimates of density and abundance than DPT,
given Depth Anything's reasonable performance without calibration and its ability to better
capture fine detail in depth contrast than DPT (Section~\ref{subsubsec:distance_comparison}).

\subsubsection{Model Calibration}

In Section~\ref{subsubsec:var_cal}, it was demonstrated that, when DPT is calibrated using
only the closest and furthest reference frame, the estimated distances of close pixels are
underestimated, while those of far pixels are overestimated.
In an attempt to explain this, a hypothesis was proposed, theorising that the true relationship
between DPT outputted disparity and metric disparity follows a non-linear mapping.
To the best of the author's knowledge, the literature contains no studies that map
DPT disparity to metric disparity.
As such, this relationship should be investigated.
Besides validating this hypothesis, evidence of non-linearity would give insight into improved
methods of DPT calibration for more robust predictions.

Additionally, better calibrations may be achieved simply through the implementation of expanded
referencing for refined calibration.
Decreasing the distance interval by which references are recorded, is likely to show some positive
effects on estimate error through minimisation of outlier influence.
Furthermore, an interesting avenue of study may be the use of multiple references per distance
interval, where these references are recorded at a variety of positions in the frame spanning its
width.

Having said this, the caveat here is that, while this may produce better estimates, the
aforementioned calibration frame preparation bottleneck would become expanded.
Consequently, these factors must be balanced accordingly to some degree; although, with the current
capabilities of monocular depth estimation technology, it follows that optimised calibrations
are most desirable as to minimise the error of distance estimates.

\subsection{Closing Remarks}

As the state of biodiversity in ecosystems around the world continues to decline, novel
and streamlined methods of large-scale conservational biomonitoring are becoming increasingly
in demand.
All in all, the results of this study provide a basis to address this, demonstrating proof-of-concept
that an appropriately implemented MDE based distance sampling pipeline can be applied in a real-world
context to give efficient assessments of animal populations at scale.
The accuracy of estimates obtained through this methodology is already approaching that of conventional
means, while simultaneously necessitating only a fraction of the manual resources.
Inevitable adoption of this framework by conservation practitioners will most certainly prove fruitful.

Nevertheless, further optimisation of this methodology is necessary before it can become convention.
Future iterations of MDE integrated distance sampling pipelines must focus on overcoming the
barrier to detection which is currently a significant roadblock to achieving estimates in line with
manual approaches.
Research should also prioritise achieving increased accuracy of distance estimates, exploring robust
calibration techniques as well as pioneering novel architectures and training protocols optimised
for MDE\@.

Recent years have seen huge advancements across the machine learning space.
Tasks once constrained by manual resources can now be automated in a faction of the time
while achieving superior results.
It is most certain that the continued application of state-of-the-art research to conservation
practices will produce impactful results, advancing technologies that will support the preservation of
biodiversity around the world.
