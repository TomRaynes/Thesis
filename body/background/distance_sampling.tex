\subsection{Distance Sampling}

Distance sampling is a statistical framework by which the local population density and abundance
of a species is estimated.
Two primary methods, 'line' and 'point' transect distance sampling,\cite{distance_sampling_applications}
exist for this; however, the methodology explored in this project leverages the latter approach,
where each camera trap is treated as a single point transect

Here, it is assumed that the true distribution of individuals in the population is evenly distributed
over the survey area.\cite{distance_samping}
In principle, point transect distance sampling surveys an area surrounding an observation point
(origin) and records instances of animal observations.
For all observations, the animal's radial distance with respect to the origin is recorded.
An assumption is made that the probability of detecting an animal at distance zero with respect to
the origin is 1 and that this probability of detection decreases as the animal's distance from the
origin increases.\cite{ds_design}
Therefore, to accurately derive an estimate of population density, the detectability of the population
with respect to distance must be modelled to account for undetected animals.

\subsubsection{Density and Abundance Derivation}

The following summarises the key steps for deriving density and abundance estimates according
to the comprehensive distance sampling methodology introduced in 'Distance Sampling: Estimating Abundance
of Biological Populations' (Buckland et al., 1993).\cite{distance_samping}

\vspace{5mm}
\textbf{The Detection Function}

The detection function, $g(r)$, describes the probability that a given animal will be detected
at radial distance $r$ from the origin.
The observed distances are grouped into distance interval bins that cover the range of observed distances
(e.g., 0–1 m, 1–2 m, \ldots) up to the furthest considered distance, $w$, known as the truncation distance.
To estimate density, a detection function is fitted to model the observed distribution of detection
distances at the transect (Figure~\ref{fig:function_fitting}.

\vspace{3mm}

The 'wellness of fit' of several detection function candidates is assessed.
A variety of candidates can be used; however, common candidates include the half-normal and
hazard-rate distributions, given by
\[
    g(r) = e^{-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}} \qquad \mathrm{and} \qquad g(r) = 1 - e^{-(\frac{r}{\sigma})^{-\beta}}
\]
respectively, where $\sigma$ and $\beta$ are the unknown parameters that maximise the wellness of fit
of the given candidate function to the distribution of observed distances.
To determine the parameterisation of a candidate function, these parameters are varied and the wellness
of fit of the specific parameterisation is assessed.

\vspace{5mm}
\textbf{The Probability Density Function}

To do this, the probability density function, $f(r)$, is derived for the parameterised candidate function,
given by
\[
    f(r) = \dfrac{r \cdot g(r)}{\displaystyle \int_{0}^{w} r \cdot g(r) \, dr} \mathrm{.}
\]
The probability density function is a measure of the distribution of observed detections, i.e.,
given an animal is detected between zero and $w$ meters, the probability of the detection distance
being at $r$ meters.

\vspace{5mm}
\textbf{Wellness of Fit Assessment}

The likelihood, $L$, of the probability density function, $f(r)$, is then calculated to determine the wellness
of fit of the corresponding detection function, $g(r)$.
This is given by
\[
    L = \prod_{i=1}^n f(r_i)\mathrm{,}
\]
where $r_i$ is the $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ observed distance and $n$ is the total number of observations.
Here, the likelihood gives a measure of the likelihood of the observed distribution of distances
arising, given that true detectability is modelled by the given candidate function.
As such, the appropriate parameters of the candidate function are determined to be those that result
in the maximisation of the corresponding likelihood, $L$.
Parameters for each of the candidate functions are determined using this approach.

Now with a set of parameterised candidate functions, the final detection function, $g(r)$, can be
determined by calculating the Akaike information criterion, AIC, for each of the candidate functions.
This is given by
\[
    \mathrm{AIC} = 2q - 2ln(L) \mathrm{,}
\]
where $L$ is the likelihood resulting from the given candidate function and $q$ is the number of
parameters of the given candidate function.
Here, $ln(L)$, is referred to as the 'log-likelihood' and gives a measure of the wellness of fit of
the given candidate function.
Conversely, $q$ is a measure of complexity of the given candidate function and contributes to a
'penalty' term in the equation.
A such, the final detection function, $g(r)$, is determined to be the candidate function that gives
the lowest AIC value.

\textbf{Density and Abundance Estimates}

Before density is calculated, the effective area, $A_e$, of the transect is
calculated.
This is given by
\[
    A_e = 2\pi \int_{0}^{w} r \cdot g(r) \, dr \mathrm{,}
\]
where $w$ is the truncation distance.\\
\textbf{Density}, $\bm{D}$, is then given by
\[
    D = \dfrac{n}{kA_e} \mathrm{,}
\]
where $n$ is the total number of observed individuals and $k$ is the total number of transects.\\
\textbf{Abundance}, $\bm{N}$, is then given by
\[
    N = D \cdot A \mathrm{,}
\]
where $D$ is density and $A$ is the total area surveyed.


\subsubsection{Software Implementation for Camera Traps}

In practice, this project processes automated distance observation data with a script used
previously by practitioners with manually estimated data.
This was supplied with the dataset (Section~\ref{subsubsec:dataset}).
The script follows the same fundamental principles; however, adaptations are made to cater
to estimates derived from camera trap video.

Firstly, like all conventional cameras, camera traps capture a specific field of view (FOV).
This is used to scale the effective area, $A_e$, of the point transect.
In this case, cameras with a FOV of 38\textdegree are used, so this is scaled to a fraction
of 38/360.
Additionally, as camera traps operate 24 hours per day, the script uses detection timestamps
with temporal operation metadata to model the behaviour of the population to derive an availability
factor (activity).
Density, $D$, then inversely scales with both availability and operation time.\cite{ct_distance_sampling}

\vspace{5mm}
\textbf{Bootstrap Calculations}

The script also performs a number of bootstrapping operations to the data.
This involves resampling the data many times (50 times in this case) and re calculating the
density and abundance estimates each time.
This gives a distribution of many estimates from which standard error and variance can be assessed.
Ultimately, this gives a more complete picture of the robustness and sensitivity of the density and
abundance estimates to random variations in the data, giving a better description of
uncertainty.\cite{distance_samping}
